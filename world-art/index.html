Europe

Chauvet Cave paintings

About 36,000 years ago · Ardèche, France

Artists: Ice Age painters, names unknown

Lions, horses, rhinoceroses, and mammoths drawn in charcoal and red ochre, using the bulges of the rock for shape and movement. Found in 1994, they are among the oldest known figurative paintings on Earth.

See it: the cave is sealed to protect it; a full-size replica, Grotte Chauvet 2, is nearby.

Oceania

Rock art of Kakadu

Tens of thousands of years, and still living · Northern Territory, Australia

Artists: generations of Aboriginal painters

Fish, turtles, and people painted in the "X-ray" style that shows bones and organs, layered on the same shelters over countless generations. It is one of the longest continuous art traditions in the world, cared for today by the Bininj/Mungguy traditional owners.

See it: Ubirr and Burrungkuy (Nourlangie) in Kakadu National Park.

Asia

Jōmon pottery

From about 14,000 BCE · Japan

Artists: Jōmon potters, names unknown

Some of the oldest pottery on Earth, named for the cord ("jōmon") pressed into the wet clay. Thousands of years later, potters in Niigata built the wild "flame-style" pots, whose rims rise like fire.

See it: Tokyo National Museum, and the Tokamachi City Museum, which holds a flame-style pot that is a National Treasure.

Africa

San rock paintings of the Drakensberg

Painted over at least 3,000 years · South Africa and Lesotho

Artists: San hunter-gatherers

Eland, hunters, dancers, and scenes from the spirit world, painted in rock shelters high in the mountains. The range holds tens of thousands of images, the largest group of rock paintings in Africa south of the Sahara.

See it: Maloti-Drakensberg Park, with guided walks to shelters like Game Pass.

Middle East

The hymns of Enheduanna

About 2285–2250 BCE · Ur, Iraq

Artist: Enheduanna, poet and high priestess

The daughter of King Sargon of Akkad, she served the moon god at Ur and wrote hymns to the goddess Inanna. She is the earliest author in the world known by name.

See it: the Disk of Enheduanna, which shows her at worship, is at the Penn Museum in Philadelphia.

Oceania

Lapita pottery

About 1500–500 BCE · from New Guinea to Samoa

Artists: Lapita potters, ancestors of today's Polynesians

Pots stamped with fine toothed patterns, including faces, made by the seafarers who first settled the remote Pacific. Following the pottery traces one of the greatest voyages in human history.

See it: museums in New Caledonia, Vanuatu, and Fiji. The name comes from the Lapita site in New Caledonia.

TAROT

Nearly 600 years of painted cards.

Tarot began in northern Italy in the 1400s as a card game, with trump cards painted by hand for noble families. Using the cards to tell fortunes came much later, in France in the 1780s. These five decks shaped how tarot looks today.

Bergamo

Visconti-Sforza tarot

About 1450s · Milan, Italy

Artist: Bonifacio Bembo (attributed)

Painted by hand, with backgrounds of gold leaf, for the ruling families of Milan. 74 cards survive, the most complete deck of its time. Four are lost, including the Devil and the Tower.

See it: 35 cards at the Morgan Library & Museum in New York, 26 at the Accademia Carrara in Bergamo, and 13 with the Colleoni family in Bergamo.

Milan

Sola Busca tarot

About 1491 · Ferrara or Venice, Italy

Artists: unknown engravers, colored by hand

The oldest complete 78-card tarot, and the first to put a scene on every card, even the number cards. Pamela Colman Smith likely saw photos of it in London in 1907, and some of its scenes show up in her famous deck.

See it: the Pinacoteca di Brera, Milan, which acquired it in 2009.

Marseille

Tarot de Marseille

1600s–1700s · Paris, Lyon and Marseille, France

Artists: card makers such as Jean Noblet, Jean Dodal and Nicolas Conver

Printed from carved woodblocks and colored through stencils in a few bold colors. This pattern became the standard, and most decks since follow its order of 22 trump cards.

See it: early decks at the Bibliothèque nationale de France in Paris. Conver’s 1760 design is still printed.

London

Rider-Waite-Smith tarot

1909 · London, England

Artist: Pamela Colman Smith, with A. E. Waite

Smith drew all 78 cards in about six months for a flat fee, giving every card a scene. It became the best-known tarot in the world, while she died poor in 1951 and her name was left off the box for decades. The card of the day below uses her 1909 art.

See it: her original drawings are lost. Early printings survive in libraries and private collections.

Figueres

Artist: Salvador Dalí

The producers of the James Bond film Live and Let Die asked Dalí to paint its tarot cards. His fee was too high, so another artist made the film’s deck, but Dalí kept going, mixing surrealism with famous old paintings. It was published in 1984.

See it: the deck is still printed. Dalí’s own museum is in Figueres, Spain.

TAROT CARD OF THE DAY

Today’s card.

Tarot can’t tell the future, and we won’t pretend it can. For centuries, though, these pictures have been a good prompt for thinking things over. Today’s card is picked by the date, so everyone sees the same one.

Card XVII

The Star

⛅ Weather in this card: a clear night full of stars

What’s painted: A woman kneels by a pool under eight bright stars, pouring water onto the land and into the pool.

Traditionally stands for: hope, renewal and calm after a storm

Think about: What gives me hope right now?
